Philosophy

A logo is a signature. A brand is a promise kept on every surface.

I don't start with a mood board. I start with the decision a brand needs to make about itself — what it will always do, and what it will never do. Everything visual follows from that: the mark, the type, the grid, the way a button asks to be pressed.

A good identity isn't decoration bolted onto a business. It's the business made visible — the same logic showing up in a business card, a product screen, and a billboard, without ever needing to explain itself twice.

  1. 01 Strategy before aesthetics Taste without a reason behind it doesn't survive contact with a real business.
  2. 02 Systems, not decorations A brand should still work in black and white, at 16 pixels, read aloud over the phone.
  3. 03 Restraint is a decision What you leave out of a brand says as much as what you put in.
Selected Works

Five briefs.
Five different problems.

Task. Give a new padel brand founded by Islam Makhachev a mark that reads as athletic without borrowing tennis or squash clichés.

Strategy. Built the identity around motion — a single continuous stroke that reads as a swing, a court line, and a monogram at once.

System. One mark, two weights of one grotesk, and a two-colour rule that had to survive court signage, jerseys, and a phone screen equally.

Task. A regional federation needed to look credible next to national sports bodies, on a fraction of the budget.

Strategy. Traded decoration for structure — a strict grid borrowed from official scoring documents became the entire visual language.

System. A badge-format mark built to survive being stamped, embroidered, and screen-printed without ever being redrawn.

Task. A graphic design course needed a site that taught by example — the design itself had to be the first lesson.

Concept. Every section demonstrates one principle it's about to explain — grid, contrast, hierarchy — before a single word of copy.

Result. Enrollment copy moved from "trust us" to "look what you'll be able to build."

Task. A consulting firm's old identity looked younger than its clients. It needed to look like advice worth paying for.

Strategy. Removed every gradient and icon from the old site. Replaced them with numbers, tables, and one accent colour used only for decisions.

System. A typographic identity — no symbol, just a name set with absolute consistency across every material.

Task. A croissant bakery competing against cafés ten times its marketing budget, on packaging alone.

Concept. Leaned into the lamination process itself — the identity's core pattern is literally the cross-section of a croissant's layers.

Result. Packaging became the storefront's main advertisement — customers started photographing the box before the pastry.

Branding Process

Five stages.
No shortcuts between them.

Audit the market, the competitors, and the client's own assumptions about who they are. Most projects lose their way here — by skipping it.

Turn research into a position: what the brand will always do, what it will never do, and the one sentence everything else has to agree with.

Explore direction wide, then commit narrow. Three concepts, each pushed far enough to actually disagree with the other two — not three shades of the same idea.

Build the system: mark, type, colour, grid, motion — and the rules that keep them coherent once someone other than the designer starts using them.

Ship the guidelines, the templates, and the first real applications together — so the brand arrives already knowing how to behave.

About

Working with brands anywhere.

Trained first as an illustrator, which is the long way of saying: I care more about whether a shape earns its place than whether it's on trend. Every project starts the same way — by trying to talk the client out of the first idea, to make sure the second one actually holds up.

Five years in, the projects that still hold up are the restrained ones — the identities that made one strong decision and kept it, rather than the ones that tried to say everything at once.
